A Foreigner Friendly Guide to Grocery Shopping in Da Nang

Where to buy food cheap, fresh, and fast, from the local markets to Go! to Moonmilk, with real prices, QR payments, and the Indian ingredient hacks.

By Nomad Ninad2026-09-21 · 8 min read

Groceries are one of the quiet reasons life in Da Nang costs me so little, but only because I learned where to shop. The gap between the cheapest and the most expensive place to buy the exact same food here is genuinely large, larger than most people expect, and knowing the map is a real part of how I live well on a lean budget. After enough months of buying food here every week, this is my actual playbook as a foreigner who lives here, ranked from the cheapest fresh food to the fastest quality grab, plus the one kind of place you should almost never do a real shop.

Local markets are unbeatable for fresh food

Nothing beats the local wet markets for fruit, vegetables, herbs, seafood, and meat. Chợ Cồn and Chợ Hàn are the big famous ones, the Bắc Mỹ An market is the handy local one near the beach where a lot of us actually live, and almost every neighborhood has its own smaller market a few minutes away. The produce is fresher than anything in a supermarket, because it often arrived that morning, and the prices are a fraction of what the same items cost with a barcode on them. A full week of fruit, vegetables, and fresh food for one person can come to around 400,000 dong, which is only 15 to 16 dollars. This is the real engine of eating cheap and well in Vietnam.

Here is the one honest catch, and it is the reason the market should not be your very first stop. Prices are not on labels, and as a foreigner you will often be quoted a higher number than a local pays, at least until you know the going rate. So learn the real prices first by doing a shop or 2 at Go!, where everything is fixed and printed, and then the markets can no longer overcharge you. A little friendly haggling helps too. You do not even need cash any more, because most stalls now take QR payments, and I pay with Moreta, an app built for travelers that lets you scan any Vietnamese QR code and pay straight from your home bank or card, with no local account needed. Go in the morning for the freshest pick, and the market quietly becomes the cheapest part of your whole month.

Go! is the all rounder for a proper weekly shop

When I want everything in one trip with prices I can trust, I go to Go!, the big supermarket on Hùng Vương that used to be called Big C. It has the widest general selection in the city at fair fixed prices, from local staples to a decent run of imported bits, and there is a proper mall attached, so it is a pleasant outing rather than a chore. You do pay for that ease, but only a little, usually around 10 to 15% more than the local market for the same items, and in return you skip the chaos, the haggling, and the foreigner pricing entirely. It is the least exciting option and the best value once you count in your time and patience. It is also the place I send every newcomer first, to learn what things actually cost.

MM Mega Market is the Costco of Da Nang

If you buy in volume, or you want western products without the small store markup, MM Mega Market is the closest thing Da Nang has to Costco. It is a big standalone store with free parking for your motorbike right out front, and inside it is bulk everything, fresh cut meat, fish, a bakery, mountains of snacks and instant noodles, produce, and a serious liquor section. The per unit prices on the things you buy a lot of are the best in the city, so it is where I stock up rather than where I grab a couple of things. Go with a plan and a big bag.

Moonmilk is the smart quick grab

Some days you do not want a full market trip, you just need a few good things fast. That is what Moonmilk is for. It is small, about the size of a convenience store, but the selection is well chosen for exactly the western basics that are hard to find elsewhere, so think good bread, real cheese, Greek yogurt, nut milks, ground coffee, craft beer, and wine. The cheese selection in particular is excellent, and for the occasional cooking project where you need a couple of specific quality ingredients, it is the best place in the city. A couple of small tips from me, their baking chocolate is genuinely good, and if you catch the croissants fresh they are outstanding. There are a few branches around the beach side of the city. It costs more than the market for anything basic, so you do not do your main shop here, but for a fast and high quality grab it beats both the trek to a big store and the generic corner shop every time.

If you miss Indian ingredients

As an Indian myself, this is the section I care about most. There is a small Gujarati grocery near the tourist strip that stocks the Indian staples no Vietnamese supermarket carries, the daals, the spices, the right flours, and all the little things that make home cooking taste right. And here is a trick worth knowing. If all you need is atta for chapatis, you do not even need a shop for it, because the Indian restaurants here will often sell it to you straight from their kitchen. I picked up a full kilo of atta from a restaurant for about 2 dollars, which nothing on a supermarket shelf here was ever going to match.

The convenience stores are the expensive trap

This is the one to be careful with. The Circle K, WinMart, and GS25 type convenience stores are on every corner, and they are consistently the most expensive place to buy anything real. They are genuinely useful for a bottle of water at midnight or a snack in an emergency, and that is what they are for. Do an actual grocery shop in one and you quietly pay a premium on every single item, sometimes several times the market price for the very same thing. Use them for a quick emergency grab, and do your real shopping anywhere else.

A few things that make it smoother

A few small habits make all of this easier. Go to the market in the morning, when the produce is freshest and the crowd is thinner. Keep a little cash on you for the smallest stalls, though most now take QR through an app like Moreta. Carry a reusable bag on your motorbike, since you will shop more often and in smaller amounts than you probably did back home. And on the days you do not feel like leaving the house at all, the delivery apps will bring a full supermarket shop to your door for a small fee, which is its own quiet luxury here.

How I actually shop

My routine is simple once you have the map. Fresh fruit, vegetables, herbs, and seafood come from the local market, and that is most of what I eat. The bigger staples and anything imported come from Go!, or a bulk run to MM Mega Market when I am stocking up. When I just need a quick few things done well, I stop at Moonmilk. The convenience stores get my money only when I am desperate and it is late.

Eating mostly from the market, one person can keep a full week of groceries around 400,000 dong, which is 15 to 16 dollars, fruit and vegetables included. For the 2 of us, cooking at home about 4 nights a week, a weekly shop usually lands near 700,000 dong, which is under 30 dollars. That is the entire food bill for the week, and it is a real piece of why the whole lean life actually works here. Frequently Asked Questions

Where do foreigners buy groceries in Da Nang?

The local wet markets, Chợ Cồn, Chợ Hàn, and the Bắc Mỹ An market, are the cheapest for fresh produce, meat, and seafood. Go! is the reliable all rounder supermarket with fixed prices, MM Mega Market is the bulk and Costco style option, and Moonmilk is the best small shop for Western imports. Skip the convenience stores like Circle K and WinMart for a real shop, because they are consistently the most expensive.

How much do groceries cost in Da Nang?

Very little. One person eating mostly from the market can keep a full week of groceries around 400,000 dong, which is about 15 to 16 dollars, fruit and vegetables included. For 2 people cooking at home about 4 nights a week, a weekly shop usually runs near 700,000 dong, under 30 dollars.

Can you pay by QR code at Da Nang markets?

Yes. Most stalls and stores now take QR payments, so you rarely need much cash. I use Moreta, an app built for travelers that lets you scan any Vietnamese QR code and pay straight from your home bank or card, with no local account needed.

Where can you buy Indian groceries in Da Nang?

There is a small Gujarati grocery near the tourist strip that carries Indian staples like daals, spices, and the right flours. For atta specifically, the Indian restaurants will often sell it to you straight from their kitchen, and I have bought a full kilo for about 2 dollars.

This is a personal account of how I shop for groceries in Da Nang, and prices, stores, and apps change over time, so treat the numbers as a rough guide and check the current details for yourself.
