Why odds, and not one number

Every calculator you've used hands you one number from a smooth average. Nobody retires into an average.

I ran a ₹10 crore retirement at a 3% withdrawal through the engine behind this planner. On an exact 11% every year it grows to ₹286 crore and gets called safe. Across 1,500 futures built from real Nifty years, with 6% inflation, only 43 in 100 last 40 years and the typical one runs out. Same inputs. The gap between those two results is the entire reason this page exists, and the thing every step of the planner is built to close.

Things people ask before they trust it
"Isn't this the 4% rule with extra steps?"+

The 4% rule was built on 30 year retirements and one country's past returns. Stop in your early 40s and you're asking your market, after your inflation, to carry 45 or 50 years. The planner doesn't assume a rule; it tests your spending against real sequences of returns and tells you how often it holds.

"My situation isn't simple."+

Good, nobody's is. A child who leaves home at 22, parents who may need help later, a property that starts paying rent at 45, part time work that might dry up early, money in two currencies. Every one of those is an input with a start and an end, and the report tells you which of them the plan depends on most.

"Where do the market numbers come from?"+

Real calendar years for the market you choose, drawn in multi-year blocks so crashes and their recoveries stay together, then centred on the return you set. It can only recombine years that have already happened, so it cannot see a crash deeper than anything on record, and it says so on the report.

"Where does my data go?"+

It stays in your browser. The link to your plan carries your inputs, which is how you come back to it or send it to someone, and the PDF is generated on your machine.
